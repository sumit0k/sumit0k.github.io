How it's worked out

Each loan's EMI is fixed once on its own rate. Interest is charged monthly. For the OD, it's charged on outstanding minus your average cash balance that month.

Each month the deposit lands on day 1. Expenses (including EMI) are a share of that income, spent across five weeks in the split you set, same as SBI MaxGain, so money spent in week 4 still counts for the first three weeks. The always-parked amount is never touched. Monthly and one-off withdrawals come out of the rest of the parked cash at the start of their month. Income stops at retirement. Leftover cash carries forward and keeps building.

Banks compute this daily, so expect a small gap versus your statement. If the account runs dry the balance stops at zero and the shortfall is assumed to be paid from elsewhere.

Break-even balance is the cash you'd need to keep parked constantly, with no other flows, for the OD to cost the same interest as the normal loan.

Things the maths leaves out

Your current lender may cut your rate for a small fee if you ask. Try that first; it often beats switching.

The FD vs SIP view ignores tax. FD interest is taxed at your slab every year; SIP gains are taxed only when you sell, at a lower rate. Counting tax would tilt it further towards switching.

The new rate is usually floating and can be revised. A lower rate today isn't locked in.

Measured at the end of your loan tenure, or later if a rate rise stretches it. While the prepay-side loan is running, all its spare money goes into that loan. Once it is paid off, the EMI it no longer pays is invested at the same return, so both sides spend the same each month. New money stops on both sides after your stop year, or when the prepay loan is paid off if that comes first; whatever is invested stays invested to the end. With "Then pay EMI from investments" ticked, EMIs after the stop year come from each side's investments first, then from your pocket.

Things the maths leaves out

When the return equals the loan rate, both come out about the same before tax. Tax on gains, and the risk that markets return less, tip it towards prepaying. Investing only wins if you reliably beat the break-even return.

Under the old tax regime, interest up to ₹2 L a year is deductible under Section 24(b), which makes the loan slightly cheaper than its rate. Prepaying also gives up the liquidity you'd keep by investing.

Actual market dynamics

Instead of one steady return, the invested money follows the Nifty 50's real yearly ups and downs from 1996 to . Year 1 of your loan gets the start year's return, year 2 the next, and so on. After the latest year the sequence runs backwards to your start year, then forwards again.
